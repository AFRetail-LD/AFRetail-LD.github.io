How to use this guide

This guide is about work you already do. It does not ask you to find extra time. Each prompt is linked to a task that already happens — the Monday review, the pre-open, month end, the renewal date, the interview.

Pick two prompts. Just two. Choose two that match tasks you do every week. Use them every time that task comes round, for three weeks. Two prompts you use often help far more than twenty you try once.

Change the words in brackets. Anything in [brackets] is for you to replace with your brand, your category, your store, your file names. Once a prompt works twice, rewrite it in your own words and save your version.

Always check the answer. Copilot is fast. It is not always right. Every prompt here tells Copilot to say when it cannot find something. Read that part first.

How to write a prompt

SPECS: five parts, every time

If an answer is not useful, one of these five is usually missing.

Situation

What you are doing, and when.

Purpose

What you need the answer for.

Evidence

Which files or emails Copilot should read.

Constraints

The rules. How long, which language, what to leave out.

Shape

What the answer should look like. A table. Six bullets. 120 words.

One prompt, split into its five parts

A weekly category review, in any business
Situation
I am going to the weekly category review for [category] in [brand].
Evidence
Read the sell-through report /[file] and last week's notes /[file].
Purpose
I need to know which five lines to defend and which five to question.
Constraints
Use only numbers in the file. If the file does not show the reason, write "not in the file". Do not guess.
Shape
One table: SKU, name, change vs last week, weeks of cover, reason, what I should do. One page only.

All the prompts in this guide are written this way. The Constraints part is what stops bad answers.

Must follow

Ten rules

Short, so you can remember them on a shop floor or in a meeting. These are set centrally. A business cannot change them.

Always give it a source

Tell Copilot which file, email or chat to read. If you do not, it will write something that sounds right but is not.

If you can see it, Copilot can see it

Copilot can open anything you have access to. That includes files someone shared by mistake. If you see something you should not, tell your manager. Do not use it.

Partner brand rules come first

IKEA, Inditex, M&S, Toys R Us and other partner brands have their own rules about data, words and designs. If their rules say no, the answer is no.

Keep costs and contracts inside

Never paste cost prices, supplier terms, contracts, leases, colleague details or customer data into a public AI tool. Use the work version of Copilot only.

Copilot is not a lawyer

It can find what we said and when. It cannot tell you what a lease, contract or policy clause means. Send those questions to Legal.

People data stays in HR systems

Pay, performance ratings, case notes and personal details about a colleague go in work Copilot only, and are stored where HR records are kept. Never anywhere else.

Check every number

Copilot can get sums wrong. Check any number against the real system before it goes into a pack, a report or an email outside your team.

Arabic needs a person

Someone who speaks Arabic must read any Arabic text before a customer sees it. A back-translation is a check, not an approval.

You own what you send

Read anything before it goes to a customer, a supplier, a landlord, a candidate or the whole business. Copilot writes the draft. You send it.

Say when you used it

If Copilot wrote most of a document other people will rely on, write that in the document.

Getting started

The first 90 days, with no new meetings

Guides usually get launched, then forgotten. This plan adds nothing to anyone's calendar. Every check-in happens inside a meeting you already have. Start with one business. Do not start all eight at once.

Days 0–14Start

Each team picks two prompts and names the meeting they belong to

The team champion reads their section, picks two prompts, and writes down which existing meeting or routine each one belongs to. One line per prompt, pinned in the team's Teams channel.

  • Name one champion for each team you actually have. Not every business has all twelve.
  • Champions try their two prompts on real work before anyone else sees the guide.
  • If a prompt does not work for the champion, remove it now.
Days 15–45Use

Use your two prompts every time the task comes round

Doing the same prompt nine times teaches you more than trying nine prompts once.

  • Take 15 minutes at the end of a meeting you already have. One person shows a prompt that gave a bad answer. The team fixes it together using SPECS.
  • Talk about what went wrong, not only what went well. That is where the learning is.
  • Champions write down which prompts people keep using, and which were tried once and dropped.
Days 46–75Build

Anything three people keep using gets built properly

If three or more people have used a prompt for three weeks running, it is worth setting up properly.

  • Pin it in the team's Copilot workspace so nobody has to type it again.
  • If the files and the format never change, build it as an agent. Then it runs without anyone remembering.
  • If a prompt needs data we do not have, that is a data problem, not a prompt problem. Write it down and send it to the platform team.
Days 76–90Hand over

Team leaders take over, and the next business starts

The guide stops being a project and becomes a working document the business owns.

  • Each team leader owns their section. They can delete prompts nobody uses.
  • One version number, one owner, one review every three months.
  • New starters get their team's two prompts in week one. Do not send them the whole guide.
  • Write down what worked and what did not before the second business starts.
Measuring it

How we know it is working

Counting licences and messages tells you nothing about whether work got better. These five tell you something.

How many tasks are covered

How many of the 66 tasks in this guide have someone actually doing them with Copilot.

70% by day 90

How many prompts people keep using

Prompts used three weeks in a row. This is the only measure that shows a prompt really fits the work.

Above 40%

Time saved, task by task

Ask once a month about one named task. Do not ask people how much time Copilot saved them in general, because they will guess.

First measure at day 15

How many edits before it is usable

If this number is not going down, the prompt is wrong, not the person.

Lower each month

Prompts built properly

Prompts turned into pinned prompts or agents. This is where the time saving adds up.

8 or more per business

Clever prompts nobody repeats

People share impressive prompts in channels and never use them again. Check how often a prompt is reused, not how many likes it got.

Only one person using it

One keen person in a team of eight doing all the work. That is not adoption. It is a single point of failure.

Sending without reading

Answers going out with no human check. The first wrong number that reaches a supplier, a landlord or a candidate sets us back a year.

Counting licences

If progress is ever reported as how many people have Copilot, we are measuring the wrong thing.

Keeping it useful

Adding your own prompts

Teams will write their own prompts, and they should. Add a prompt to this guide when all six of these are true.

  1. It is for a task that already happens in someone's week.
  2. It tells Copilot which file, email, chat or meeting to read.
  3. It says what the answer should look like, clearly enough that two people would get a similar result.
  4. It tells Copilot what to do when the files do not have the answer.
  5. Someone other than the writer has used it on their own work and got something useful.
  6. It is faster than doing the task by hand. Time it once. Do not assume.

Prompts nobody uses are deleted at the three-month review. A guide that only gets longer is a guide nobody reads.
